Side quest 01 · Perfume shop

Giving an Instagram perfume shop a front door.

Biba sells perfume from Shop 6 in Jos and from her Instagram DMs. Her buyers rarely know what a perfume is called. They describe it: something sweet for my wife, under 30k. I designed and built a website that sells the way she already does.

What 545 posts said.

Before designing anything I read every post on her Instagram. Product shots, the bottles on a shelf, pulled a handful of likes. Posts with Biba in them pulled ten times that.

That changed the brief. The site speaks in her voice, every pick is framed as hers, and every order lands in her own WhatsApp, not in an inbox nobody answers.

Four things the shop was up against.

  • P.01Nobody knows the namesBuyers ask for a mood and a budget, not “Khamrah Qahwa”. A search box that needs the exact name is a dead end.
  • P.02Most stock isn’t on the shelf307 of the 408 perfumes are ordered in, so their prices are estimates, not promises.
  • P.03Nobody pays a strangerA card form on an unknown website asks for trust the shop hasn’t earned there yet. Her DMs already have it.
  • P.04Every question is a DMDo you have it, how much, is it like Baccarat, can you send it to Abuja. Each one waits for Biba to reply by hand, and a buyer who has to wait may not come back.

Ask the shop, not the shopkeeper.

The site’s real job is to answer the questions buyers would otherwise DM, so Biba’s time goes to orders, not to typing prices. The hero is one question: describe the scent you’re looking for. The search reads a budget (“under 30k”), an occasion, who it’s for and a scent family, explains each match in one line, and offers one-tap refinements: cheaper, sweeter, fresher, for him, for her. It never returns nothing. With no exact match it shows the closest picks and says so.

A tour of the shop.

Seven screens from the live site at phone width, because buyers arrive by tapping a link in Instagram or WhatsApp.

A dupe guide, not a dupe shop.

A lot of perfume shopping starts from a designer scent someone already loves. Instead of pretending to stock those, the site has a guide for each of the favourites: the original, its price, and what in the shop is closest. It says plainly that these are inspired by, not copies.

The checkout already existed.

It’s called WhatsApp. Instead of building payments and accounts, the bag turns a list of perfumes into one numbered message with references, quantities, the delivery city and the search that led there. Biba confirms stock and the total in the chat, the way she always has. The tradeoff: no on-site conversion data. So every WhatsApp tap is counted by where it happened instead.

408 perfumes, 129 photos.

A catalogue of grey placeholders would read as an empty shop. So I built a small photo pipeline: pull the brand’s catalogue shots, draw them onto a canvas, flood-fill the white background away, and check every bottle by eye. 129 made it. Three didn’t: a lifestyle shot and two bottles still in their boxes.

The other 279 needed a face that wasn’t a placeholder or a copied bottle. So I drew a system instead: eight silhouettes, 26 liquids and five metal caps, chosen from each perfume’s name and scent family.

One action per card.

The first build put two buttons on every card: Order on WhatsApp and Add to bag. On a phone, that’s 48 buttons fighting 24 bottles, and the labels wrapped. A browsing grid is for choosing, so I gave each card one quick add on the photo. WhatsApp moved to the product page, where the decision happens. A tick confirms the add, and tapping it again opens the bag instead of adding a duplicate.

Built to be found.

Every perfume has its own page and its own link preview card, so a link shared in a chat shows the bottle and the price. Delivery pages for Abuja, Kaduna and Kano answer what buyers there actually ask, and the sitemap and structured data are generated from the same catalogue as the pages.

What’s next, already prototyped.

The site runs on a catalogue file, which is fine for launch but means Biba can’t change a price without me. The next two pieces hand the shop back to her. Neither is live yet, so they’re marked as prototypes.

In closing

Scents by Biba went from a shop you could only reach through DMs to one you can browse, search in plain words and order from in a single message, without asking buyers to trust anything new. Next:

  • 01Ship the price sheet, so Biba can change a price or mark something sold out herself.
  • 02Build the Instagram agent once her post archive arrives, with approval in WhatsApp.
  • 03Measure which placement gets the most WhatsApp taps: cards, product pages or the bag.
  • 04Replace the drawn bottles with photos from her own shelf.
